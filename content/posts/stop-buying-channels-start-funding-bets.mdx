Most marketing plans still read like shopping lists. Paid search gets a line item. Social gets a line item. Content gets whatever is left. That is how teams stay busy and still miss the quarter.

A better framing: you are not buying channels. You are **funding bets** under uncertainty. Each bet has a thesis, a cost of learning, and an explicit point where you double down or shut it off.

## The portfolio, not the pie chart

Channel mix slides imply permanence. Portfolios imply management.

Treat every meaningful spend or capacity allocation as one of three bet types:

1. **Core** — proven demand capture or brand systems that must not go dark. Optimize ruthlessly; do not “innovate” them into fragility.
2. **Growth** — hypotheses with a credible path to becoming core within two to four learning cycles.
3. **Option** — cheap probes into new audiences, formats, or partners. Cap the downside. Harvest the insight even when revenue is zero.

If everything is labeled strategic, nothing is. Cap options. Protect core. Make growth bets earn promotion with evidence, not storytelling.

## Write the thesis before the brief

Before a campaign launches, force a one-paragraph thesis:

> We believe [audience] will [action] when we [intervention], because [insight]. We will know within [window] by watching [leading metric] and [lagging metric]. Kill if [threshold].

That paragraph is the asset. Creative, media, and landing pages are implementations of it. When results disappoint, you diagnose the thesis — not the agency’s slide deck.

## Kill criteria are kindness

Teams fear kill criteria because they sound punitive. They are the opposite. Without them, weak bets consume calendar, budget, and political capital indefinitely.

Good kill criteria are:

- **Time-boxed** — e.g. two full learning cycles, not “until leadership loses interest”
- **Metric-specific** — leading indicators first (qualified pipeline, retained attention, sales-accepted opportunities), vanity second
- **Owned** — one decision-maker named in the operating rhythm

If you cannot name who is allowed to stop a bet, you do not have a portfolio. You have a graveyard of half-commitments.

## A weekly scorecard that fits on one page

Daily dashboards create noise. Weekly CMO scorecards create judgment. Keep four panels:

| Panel | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Demand | Are we creating qualified opportunity at a cost we can defend? |
| Brand | Are we earning memory and preference in the accounts that matter? |
| Efficiency | Where is spend or capacity producing learning vs. ritual? |
| Risk | What will break if we keep today’s mix for 90 days? |

Each panel gets one number, one note, and one decision. That is enough. More detail belongs in channel reviews, not the executive view.

## Content is a channel bet too

Owned content fails when it is treated as a publishing obligation instead of a distribution system. Fund content like a growth bet: thesis on audience jobs-to-be-done, distribution plan before draft, and a kill/iterate rule tied to assisted pipeline or sales usage — not pageviews alone.

If sales never cites your work in deals, you are running a magazine, not a go-to-market asset.

## Decision checklist

Use this before the next planning cycle:

- [ ] Every material channel has a written thesis and kill criteria
- [ ] Budget is labeled core / growth / option — and options are capped
- [ ] One owner can stop a failing bet without a committee
- [ ] Weekly scorecard fits on one page and drives at least one decision
- [ ] Content has a distribution and sales-usage plan, not only a calendar

Channels are inventory. Bets are strategy. Fund accordingly.
